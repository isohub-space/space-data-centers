# EP Think Tank — "What if AI data centres were put in space?"

## Reported (what the source says)
- Data centres use **1.5%** of global electricity (**2%** in the EU), growing **12%/yr**; Ireland's data centres > **20%** of national electricity.
- Launch: today "several thousand euros per kilo"; ESA target **€280/kg** with a new super-heavy launcher.
- A **1 GW** orbital facility could need "**upwards of 10,000 tons**" — more than **three times** the total mass launched to orbit in 2025; "upwards of one hundred launches"; annual replacements needed at satellite end-of-life.
- Starcloud example: a **5 GW** facility's solar array "up to **4 km per side**"; an ISS-scale comparison of **60 m² and 28 kW**.
- ASCEND (Horizon Europe) "concluded a feasibility study, aiming towards an operational system from **2030**."
- Legal: space data centres sit in a "grey zone" — needs a "digital flag state" concept under the Outer Space Treaty, GDPR third-country transfer clarification, and extending **EU Space Act** definitions to ground-origin data processed in orbit. Orbital congestion regulated only indirectly via spectrum.
- Sustainability: upper-atmosphere pollution from de-orbiting "poorly understood"; lifetime emissions may exceed ground facilities.
- Search snippet (not opened): EU Space Act final adoption unlikely before **2028**, effect ~**2030**; it mandates debris-mitigation planning, collision-avoidance subscription, light/radio pollution design standards.

## Derived (our arithmetic — formula shown)
- 10,000 t per GW = **10 kg/kW** — far below Turyshev's 34–59 kg/kW; the EP figure is a floor, not an estimate. 10,000 t ÷ 200 t = **50 Starship flights**, ÷ 44 t demonstrated = **227 flights**.
- "Three times 2025 orbital mass" implies ~3,300 t launched globally in 2025.

## Relevance to the Entrant
- GDPR "third-country transfer" ambiguity for data processed in orbit is a **European compliance moat** the Entrant can design for (EU-flagged spacecraft, EU ground segment) — a real differentiator versus US operators.
- The EU Space Act timeline (2028–2030) means the Entrant's first-generation payloads fly under national law; licensing strategy should pick the member state deliberately.
