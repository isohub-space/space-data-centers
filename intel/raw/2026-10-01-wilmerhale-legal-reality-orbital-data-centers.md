# WilmerHale — "Not Just Science Fiction: The Legal Reality of Orbital Data Centers"

## Reported (what the source says)
**FCC docket state (as of 2026-10-01)**
- **Four orbital-data-centre applications accepted for filing** by the FCC: one for up to **1,000,000**
  satellites, three for about **88,000**, **51,600** and **100,000** *(reported; the alert does not name the
  applicants — the sizes match SpaceX, Starcloud, Blue Origin and Orbital in the vault)*.
- FCC Space Bureau acceptance **DA 26-113 (2026-02-04)**; FCC **Space Modernization Order FCC 26-47**, SB
  Docket 25-306 (released **2026-07-23**); an **FCC NEPA determination dated 2026-09-30**; an environmental
  petition seeking a programmatic EIS filed **2026-07-08** *(reported)*.
- Office of Space Commerce: voluntary **pilot certification for novel in-space activities**, notice 91 Fed.
  Reg. 53849 (2026-08-20), expressions of interest due **2026-10-05** *(reported)*.

**Legal issues listed**
- US licensing split: FAA (launch/re-entry, payload review), FCC (spectrum, operations, debris), Office of Space
  Commerce (remote sensing; novel activities); possible authorisation gaps for activities no programme covers.
- Export control, national-security and foreign-investment review.
- International law: Outer Space Treaty Art. VI (state responsibility) and VIII (registry jurisdiction);
  Registration and Liability Conventions.
- Data: **GDPR extraterritorial reach (Art. 3(1)–(2))** and the **US CLOUD Act (18 U.S.C. § 2713)** apply
  regardless of the server being in orbit; also data-localisation, sanctions, emerging AI rules.
- Insurance gaps across property, cyber, tech E&O, launch and in-orbit policies; contracts must allocate
  licensing delay, launch failure, capacity loss, collision, outage, data loss, early termination;
  end-of-life (deorbit, replacement, ownership transfer, disposal).
- A compute failure can become a service-interruption, contract or financing problem even when the spacecraft
  is intact *(reported)*.

## Primary (what the underlying paper / filing says — where it differs)
- Not opened: FCC DA 26-113, FCC 26-47, the 2026-09-30 NEPA determination, GAO-26-109012 (2026-04-28, "Science &
  Tech Spotlight: Data Centers in Space"). The content of the NEPA determination (categorical exclusion or not)
  is **not stated** in the alert and is the single most consequential open item for large filers.
- The New Space Economy summary (2026-10-06, https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/10/06/orbital-data-centers-and-the-law/)
  adds that GAO distinguishes **smaller systems that process space-generated data** from large facilities
  meant to compete with terrestrial data centres *(reported, via secondary; GAO not opened)*.

## Relevance to the Entrant
- **Sovereignty is not location.** GDPR and the CLOUD Act both follow the *controller*, not the server, so
  "your data never touches US soil" is not a sufficient sovereign pitch on its own; the Entrant's sovereign
  argument has to rest on a European operator, European state of registry, and EU-law contracts.
- The GAO's split between processing space-generated data and competing with ground data centres is the same
  line this vault draws; the Entrant sits on the side that regulators already treat as the smaller, more
  tractable case.
- Contracts: buyers will ask what is purchased (insight latency? compute-hours?) and what the remedy is.
  Defining the SLA in time-to-insight with service credits is a concrete design item.
